You've just heard from your classmates about Witmer, Jones, and how their work built the foundation of modern clinical psychology. Now it's your turn!

Would you rather — 1 of 2
You're a trainee at a community mental health clinic. A 9-year-old is referred for severe school refusal — three weeks of daily meltdowns at drop-off, stomach aches, crying. The presenting problem looks like a mix of separation anxiety and a possible specific phobia of the school bathroom after one bad incident six months ago. Your supervisor gives you the first session.

Do you spend the first session building a full picture — talking to parents, requesting teacher input, coordinating with the pediatrician — or do you start a structured behavioral intervention on the bathroom fear right now, because every week of avoidance is strengthening the phobia?

It depends.

And that's not a cop-out — it's the real clinical answer. Option A is cautious and Witmer would approve: you can't treat what you don't fully understand, and the bathroom fear might be masking a trauma response that exposure could actually worsen. Option B is empirically grounded: avoidance does maintain phobias, and delay has real costs when a kid is missing school every day.

Most modern evidence-based protocols do a brief functional assessment first, then intervene fast. The Witmer/Jones split isn't always sequential — it's often simultaneous. But if you had to lead with one? That's the real judgment call — and it changes case by case.

Would you rather — 2 of 2
You're presenting a case to your supervision group. Your client has a documented specific phobia significantly impairing their functioning. You've done a thorough biopsychosocial intake and have a clear picture. Your supervisor asks: "What's your treatment plan?"

A colleague argues for systematic desensitization — graduated exposure with relaxation training, rooted directly in Jones's work. Another argues for inhibitory learning exposure — no relaxation, just violating the expectancy of danger, the current gold standard. Your supervisor wants your opinion. Which do you back?

It depends — but this one leans toward B.

Craske et al. (2014) argue that relaxation during exposure can become a safety behavior, reducing the inhibitory learning that makes exposure stick long-term. The client learns "I can handle this when I'm calm" — not "this isn't actually dangerous." That's a meaningful difference for relapse prevention.

Jones was right that graduated exposure works — but the mechanism she thought was driving it (counter-conditioning) may not be the whole story. The field has evolved past her specific method while keeping her core insight. That's how science is supposed to work.

A child psychologist receives a referral for a 7-year-old with reading difficulties and behavioral outbursts in class. Before scheduling any therapy, she requests a full psychoeducational evaluation, meets with the child's teacher and principal, consults with the pediatrician about a possible ADHD diagnosis, and asks the school social worker to assess the home environment. Only after all reports are in does she write a treatment recommendation.

Witmer — and pretty purely so. The multidisciplinary team assembled before any intervention is Witmer's 1896 clinic, nearly verbatim. Psychologist, physician, teacher, social worker. The instinct to understand the whole child before acting is his signature move. Jones doesn't show up here — there's no behavioral intervention anywhere in this scenario.

Spot the legacy — 2 of 3
This one is trickier — read carefully.

A therapist is treating a 16-year-old with a phobia of vomiting. She designs a graduated treatment plan: the teen watches videos of people feeling nauseous, then spins in a chair to induce mild dizziness, then eats slightly more than comfortable at session. There is no relaxation training. The goal is for the teen to learn that nausea doesn't mean vomiting is imminent, and that they can tolerate the sensation. The therapist explicitly tells the client not to use coping strategies during exposure.

Jones — but with an important asterisk. This is exposure therapy, which descends directly from Jones's direct conditioning work. But notice what's missing: the pleasant counter-stimulus. This is the inhibitory learning model — the field evolved beyond Jones's specific mechanism while keeping her core insight that graduated exposure to the feared stimulus is the treatment. Her legacy is in the structure; the theory has been updated.

Spot the legacy — 3 of 3
Last one — and the hardest.

A clinical psychologist at a hospital-based outpatient clinic receives a referral for a 35-year-old with panic disorder. She starts with a structured diagnostic interview, consults with the referring psychiatrist about medications, and reviews previous treatment records. She then designs a 12-session CBT protocol: psychoeducation about the panic cycle, interoceptive exposure, and situational exposure to avoided places. She coordinates with the client's primary care physician throughout treatment.

Both — and this is what modern clinical psychology actually looks like. The diagnostic interview + physician coordination + records review = Witmer's multidisciplinary assessment model. The interoceptive and situational exposure protocol = Jones's direct conditioning lineage, updated through Wolpe to modern CBT. Neither pioneer alone gets you here. You need both — and that's not a coincidence. It's the inheritance.
